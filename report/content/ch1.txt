@chapter INTRODUCTION {ch:intro}

@section BACKGROUND {sec:background}

A deepfake is an image or video in which a person's face has been replaced, re-animated or generated by a machine learning model. The first widely circulated deepfakes, posted online in 2017, were face swaps produced with autoencoders. Since then the tools have become easier to use and the results more convincing. Generative adversarial networks \cite{goodfellow2014gan} and, more recently, diffusion models \cite{ho2020ddpm,rombach2022ldm} can produce faces that untrained viewers cannot tell apart from photographs, and open-source face-swapping software runs on a consumer graphics card. Surveys of the field \cite{tolosana2020survey,mirsky2021survey} group the manipulations into four families: identity swap, in which the face of one person is placed on another; expression swap or reenactment, in which the expressions of a source actor drive the face of a target; attribute editing, which changes properties such as age or hair; and entire face synthesis, in which a face of a person who does not exist is generated from noise or from a text prompt.

The same technology that supports film production and privacy-preserving data generation is also used to deceive. Manipulated videos of public figures have been used for political disinformation, synthetic faces have been used in attempts to pass video-based identity checks, and synthetic intimate imagery has been used to harass private individuals. Because a convincing forgery can be produced in minutes, manual review cannot keep pace, and automatic detection has become a necessary part of content moderation, journalism and digital forensics.

A deepfake detector is a classifier that receives a face image or a video and outputs the probability that it has been manipulated. Early detectors were convolutional networks trained on a single benchmark such as FaceForensics++ \cite{rossler2019faceforensics}. They reached accuracies close to 100% on test videos produced by the same manipulation methods but lost much of that accuracy on datasets produced by other methods, for example Celeb-DF \cite{li2020celebdf} or the Deepfake Detection Challenge data \cite{dolhansky2019dfdcpreview}. This drop, usually called the generalization problem, has been the central difficulty of the field for several years \cite{yan2023deepfakebench}.

Detectors built on large pretrained vision models have narrowed the gap considerably. CLIP \cite{radford2021clip}, an image and text encoder trained on hundreds of millions of image and caption pairs, learns visual features that are not tied to any particular manipulation. GenD \cite{yermakov2026gend} is a recent detector of this kind. It keeps the weights of the CLIP ViT-L/14 image encoder frozen and trains only the parameters of its layer-normalization layers and a linear classification head, about 0.03% of the weights, together with objectives that arrange the features on a hypersphere. GenD reports state-of-the-art average cross-dataset performance over fourteen benchmarks, which makes it a natural starting point for this project.

@section MOTIVATION {sec:motivation}

Better generalization does not remove the need to update a detector. New generators and face-swapping tools appear every few months, and an organization that operates a detector will collect examples of each new forgery as it encounters them. It will want the detector to learn these examples. The authors of GenD state this limitation themselves: their detector is trained once, and new forgery methods would require complete retraining.

Complete retraining is often not possible. The datasets used to train a detector are distributed under research licences that may forbid keeping copies indefinitely, and they contain faces of real people, which privacy regulations treat as personal data. An operator may also simply lack the original training data and the computing resources to repeat the full training. A practical update therefore has to work from a small number of new examples, it should not require storing data from earlier training stages, and it should not change the architecture or the cost of inference. In machine learning terms this is continual learning in a few-shot and exemplar-free setting.

The well-known risk of continual learning is catastrophic forgetting \cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}: when a network is trained on new data, it tends to lose what it learned before. Previous work on continual deepfake detection \cite{kim2021cored,pan2023dfil,zhang2025gpl} has mostly measured forgetting on the datasets that the detector has already learned. For a generalizable detector, however, the most valuable property is its ability to detect forgeries that it was never trained on, and an update could damage this ability while the metrics on the trained datasets still look acceptable. Whether this happens, how large the damage is, and how to prevent it had not been studied for foundation-model detectors when this project started.

A possible remedy came from a different modality. In continual detection of spoofed speech, Ma et al. \cite{ma2021dfwf} observed that genuine recordings are more consistent across conditions than spoofed ones, and proposed to keep the embeddings of genuine utterances close to those of the previous model while the model learns new attacks. Faces have the same structure: every dataset contains genuine faces, while each manipulation method produces its own kind of artifact. This project asks whether constraining only the genuine class protects generalization in a visual foundation-model detector, and why it would.

@section PROBLEM STATEMENT {sec:problem-statement}

The problem addressed by this project is the following. A deepfake detector has been trained on a base dataset and generalizes well to manipulations that it has never seen. It then receives a stream of new datasets, and each of them is available only as a small sample of 25 genuine and 25 manipulated videos. After each new dataset the detector must be updated so that it learns the new manipulations, keeps its performance on the datasets learned earlier, and above all keeps its ability to detect manipulations that do not occur anywhere in the stream. The update may not store data from earlier stages, and it may not change the architecture of the detector or its cost at inference time. The project measures how much an ordinary update damages generalization, evaluates whether a regularizer that keeps the features of genuine faces stable prevents the damage, and investigates why it does.

@section OBJECTIVES {sec:objectives}

The project had seven objectives. The first was to measure the cost of updating a generalizable foundation-model detector, both on the datasets it has learned and on manipulations it has never seen, including the effect on its decision threshold. The second was to implement genuine-sample alignment, alone and combined with logit distillation, together with a set of established continual learning methods, on the same detector and in a single code base, so that all methods are trained with the same parameters, data and budget.

The third objective was to evaluate these methods under a controlled protocol: identity-disjoint data splits, separate development and confirmation stages, two unseen datasets that were sealed before any model was evaluated on them, and hypotheses and decision rules that were written down before each set of experiments. The fourth was to test whether any benefit is specific to the genuine class, using control methods that constrain the same number of faces chosen at random or chosen from the manipulated class, and to give a mathematical argument for the observed behavior.

The fifth objective was to compare the approach with GPL \cite{zhang2025gpl}, the most closely related published method for preserving generalization in continual deepfake detection. The sixth was to test whether the result holds across training seeds, task orders, a second foundation encoder, image degradations, different amounts of training data, a longer stream of nine updates, and competitors tuned to learn as much as the proposed update. The seventh was to release the code, the data splits and the experimental records so that the study can be reproduced.

@section SCOPE OF THE PROJECT {sec:scope}

The project is limited to the detection of manipulated faces in videos and images, framed as a binary decision between genuine and manipulated. All experiments use the GenD detector, in its CLIP ViT-L/14 version and, for one replication, in its Perception Encoder version. The training stream is built from four public video datasets, FaceForensics++, DFDC-Preview, the DeepFakeDetection set and Celeb-DF, and generalization is measured on two further datasets, WildDeepfake and DeepFakeFace, which are never used for training or model selection. Each update sees between 5 and 25 videos per class.

Several related problems are outside the scope of the project. It does not develop a new detector architecture and does not train detectors from scratch. It does not address audio deepfakes, manipulations of non-face content, or the generation of new deepfakes. It does not study updates with the full training data of each new dataset, and it does not build a deployment system or a user interface. All experiments were run on a single consumer graphics card (NVIDIA RTX 5060 Ti with 16 GB of memory), which bounded the number of training runs that could be performed.

@section CONTRIBUTIONS {sec:contributions}

The project makes four main contributions. First, it shows that the cost of updating a generalizable detector is hidden from the usual continual learning metrics. Sequential few-shot fine-tuning of GenD learns every new dataset and forgets almost nothing of the earlier ones, yet it reduces detection of a manipulation that was never trained, FaceShifter, by 7.8 AUROC points, and it lowers the accuracy of the base task at a fixed threshold from 95.0% to 79.9%.

Second, it shows that genuine-sample alignment, a regularizer introduced for fake-audio detection, transfers to visual foundation-model detectors. Combined with logit distillation, it keeps the score on two sealed unseen datasets within 0.19 AUROC points of the never-updated detector on average over five training seeds, while plain fine-tuning loses 2.77 points. The result holds over three task orders, a second encoder, thirteen image degradations and a stream of nine updates, and the update outperforms a re-implementation of GPL in every setting tested.

Third, it explains the effect. Control methods that constrain the same number of faces chosen at random or from the manipulated class do not reproduce the benefit, so it is tied to the genuine class rather than to a weaker constraint. A ranking-margin argument shows why: an update can damage the detection of an unseen manipulation only through genuine and manipulated pairs with a small score margin, and only by an amount governed by how far the genuine scores move.

Fourth, the project documents problems in the base model and the benchmarks that affect any continual evaluation built on them: part of GenD's published cross-dataset performance depends on checkpoint selection with data from the target family, the widely used DeepfakeBench configuration of the DeepFakeDetection set places every video in the training, validation and test sets at the same time, the genuine and manipulated videos of Celeb-DF come from different sources unless the split is built carefully, and the usual face crops of two datasets depend on the label. The code, splits and experimental records are publicly available, and a manuscript describing the study has been prepared for journal submission.

@section ORGANIZATION OF THE REPORT {sec:organization}

The rest of the report is organized as follows. Chapter 2 reviews the literature on deepfake generation, deepfake datasets, detection methods, foundation-model detectors and continual learning, and identifies the gap that this project addresses. Chapter 3 formalizes the problem and describes the base detector, the update objective, the continual protocol, a ranking-margin analysis and the baseline methods. Chapter 4 describes the implementation, the datasets and splits, the preprocessing, the training configuration and the statistical protocol. Chapter 5 presents and discusses the results. Chapter 6 states the conclusions and outlines future work. The appendices contain additional results, the registered experimental decisions and the source code of the update step.
