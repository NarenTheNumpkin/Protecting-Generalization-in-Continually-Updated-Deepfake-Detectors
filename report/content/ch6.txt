@chapter CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK {ch:conclusion}

@section CONCLUSION {sec:conclusion}

This project studied how a generalizable deepfake detector built on a vision foundation model should be updated when new manipulations appear and only a few examples of them are available. The detector was GenD, which tunes the layer-normalization parameters of a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder. The project built an evaluation in which the detector is updated on a stream of four public datasets and its generalization is measured on two further datasets that were sealed before any model was scored on them, with identity-disjoint splits, separate development and confirmation stages, and hypotheses written down before every set of experiments.

The first finding is that the cost of updating is real but hidden. Few-shot sequential fine-tuning learns every new dataset and forgets almost nothing of the earlier ones, so by the usual continual learning metrics it succeeds. At the same time it reduces detection of a manipulation that was never trained by 7.8 AUROC points in development, lowers the unseen-panel score by 2.77 points on average over five seeds, and moves the operating point of the base task so far that its accuracy at a fixed threshold drops by more than ten points. A team that monitors only the datasets it has trained on would not notice either effect.

The second finding is that genuine-sample alignment, an objective introduced for continual fake-audio detection, transfers to visual face forgery detection and largely prevents this damage. Combined with logit distillation, it keeps the unseen-panel score within 0.19 points of the never-updated detector on average over five seeds, while the new datasets are still learned, no data is stored, no parameters are added and inference is unchanged. The advantage over plain fine-tuning, LwF, symmetric distillation and a re-implementation of GPL holds in every setting tested: five training seeds, three task orders, a second foundation encoder, thirteen image degradations, three data regimes and a stream of nine updates, where it grows to more than five points over plain fine-tuning. It also holds when the competitors are tuned on development data to learn as much, and on the main stream alignment alone with a development-selected weight does at least as well as the combination.

The third finding concerns the reason. Control methods that constrain the same number of randomly chosen or manipulated faces do not reproduce the benefit, so it is tied to the genuine class. The ranking-margin analysis explains why this should be so: an update can only damage the detection of an unseen manipulation through genuine and manipulated pairs with a small score margin, and only by an amount governed by how far the genuine scores move, which is exactly what the alignment term controls. The experiments confirm the two observable consequences of this argument: the constraint reduces the drift of genuine faces from datasets that no model saw, and the pairs lost by an update are concentrated at small margins.

Measured against the objectives of Section {sec:objectives}, the project met its goals of quantifying the cost of updates, implementing and comparing the methods under a controlled protocol, explaining the mechanism, comparing with GPL and testing generality, and it released its code and records. It did not find an update that fully protects generalization. Fully synthetic text-to-image faces still lose 4.7 points even with the best update, the new datasets are learned less completely than with plain fine-tuning, and calibration on the trained datasets is worse. Decision thresholds selected on development data did not transfer to unseen data for any method. These are not failures of the experiments but limits of the approach, and they are reported as such. Along the way the project also found problems in the base model and the benchmarks that matter for anyone who builds on them: checkpoint selection that inflates cross-dataset scores, a widely reused configuration that evaluates DFD on its own training videos, a source confound in Celeb-DF, and face crops that depend on the label.

The insight that was not obvious at the outset is that, for a detector that generalizes, the genuine class plays a special role during updates. It is the only class shared by every task and by every unseen comparison, so keeping it stable protects forgeries that the update never sees, while the manipulated classes can be left free to learn.

@section FUTURE WORK {sec:future}

Several directions follow directly from the limitations of this project. The most important is to test streams of genuinely new generators. The stream used here consists of face-swap and reenactment datasets released between 2019 and 2020, and the largest remaining loss concerns diffusion-generated faces; a stream that adds diffusion-based and commercial generators in the order in which they appeared would test the approach where it is weakest. A second direction is to extend the study to full-data updates and to other parameter-efficient detectors, such as adapter-based and low-rank methods, to see whether the role of the genuine class is the same when other parts of the encoder are tuned.

A third direction is calibration. No update rule tested here keeps a decision threshold valid on new domains, and recalibration on the newest data alone made unseen data worse. Methods that preserve calibration without stored data, for example by constraining the head together with the genuine features, or by keeping a small set of statistics of the genuine score distribution, would make continual updating much more practical. A fourth direction is to vary the base training: all experiments here start from one GenD model per encoder, and repeating the study over several independently trained base models would separate the variability of the update from that of the base. Finally, the alignment weight could be set adaptively for each update, according to how far the new data is from the genuine distribution, instead of being fixed for the whole stream.
