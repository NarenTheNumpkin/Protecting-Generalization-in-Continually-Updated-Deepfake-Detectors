@chapter LITERATURE SURVEY {ch:lit}

This chapter reviews the work that the project builds on. It starts with the methods used to create face forgeries and the datasets used to study them, then covers detection methods, from early convolutional detectors to detectors built on foundation models. The second half of the chapter covers continual learning in general, its application to deepfake detection, and the related work on continual fake-audio detection from which the update objective studied in this project originates. The chapter ends with a summary of the surveyed work and the research gap.

@section DEEPFAKE GENERATION METHODS {sec:generation}

Face forgeries are produced by four broad families of methods \cite{tolosana2020survey,mirsky2021survey}. Identity swapping replaces the face of a target person with that of a source person while keeping the target's pose, expression and lighting. The original Deepfakes method trains an autoencoder with a shared encoder and one decoder per identity: a face of person A is encoded and then decoded with the decoder of person B, and the result is blended back into the frame. FaceSwap is a lighter, graphics-based alternative that fits a 3D face model to facial landmarks and transfers the texture. FaceShifter \cite{li2020faceshifter} improved the fidelity of identity swaps with an adaptive attention mechanism that integrates identity and attribute features and a second stage that recovers occluded regions.

Expression swapping, or facial reenactment, keeps the identity of the target and transfers the expressions of a source actor. Face2Face \cite{thies2016face2face} fits a parametric face model to both videos in real time and re-renders the target face with the expressions of the source. NeuralTextures \cite{thies2019neuraltextures} learns a neural texture together with a deferred rendering network and produces reenactments with fewer visible artifacts. Reenactment changes less of the face than an identity swap, and NeuralTextures in particular is among the hardest manipulations of FaceForensics++ to detect, which the per-manipulation results of this project confirm (Section {sec:permanip}).

The third and fourth families, attribute editing and entire face synthesis, have changed most in recent years. Generative adversarial networks \cite{goodfellow2014gan} made it possible to generate high-resolution faces of people who do not exist. Denoising diffusion models \cite{ho2020ddpm} and latent diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion \cite{rombach2022ldm} now generate faces from a text prompt, or fill a masked region of a real photograph (inpainting) with content that matches its surroundings. These methods leave different traces from the blending boundaries and temporal flicker of classical face swaps. A detector trained only on face swaps may therefore miss them, which is exactly what the experiments of this project observe on the text-to-image subset of DeepFakeFace (Section {sec:main}).

@section DEEPFAKE DATASETS AND BENCHMARKS {sec:datasets-lit}

FaceForensics++ \cite{rossler2019faceforensics} is the most widely used training set. It contains 1000 original videos collected from YouTube and four manipulated versions of each, produced with Deepfakes, Face2Face, FaceSwap and NeuralTextures, at three compression levels; the medium-compression version, c23, is the usual choice. The dataset was later extended with FaceShifter videos and with the DeepFakeDetection set (DFD) \cite{dufour2019dfd}, which Google and Jigsaw recorded with 28 paid and consenting actors and which contains 363 original and 3068 manipulated videos.

Celeb-DF \cite{li2020celebdf} was built to be harder. Its second version contains 590 interview videos of 59 celebrities and 5639 deepfakes produced with an improved synthesis process that reduces color mismatch and temporal flicker. The Deepfake Detection Challenge preview dataset (DFDC-P) \cite{dolhansky2019dfdcpreview} contains about five thousand videos recorded with 66 paid actors and manipulated with two methods, which the dataset calls A and B. WildDeepfake \cite{zi2020wilddeepfake} differs from all of these because it was not produced by the dataset authors: it contains 7314 face sequences extracted from 707 deepfake videos collected from the internet, with the varied quality and unknown generation methods of real-world content. DeepFakeFace \cite{song2023deepfakeface} contains 30,000 real face images from the IMDB-WIKI collection and 90,000 manipulated images produced with three generators: Stable Diffusion inpainting, the InsightFace face-swapping tool and Stable Diffusion text-to-image generation.

DeepfakeBench \cite{yan2023deepfakebench} unified the preprocessing, training and evaluation of many detectors on these datasets and is the standard reference for cross-dataset comparisons. Its configuration files are reused by much of the later work, including the continual detection studies discussed below. During this project we found that its configuration for the DFD set places every DFD video in the training, validation and test lists at the same time (Section {sec:splits}), so that results that train on DFD with this configuration are evaluated on their own training videos.

@section DEEPFAKE DETECTION METHODS {sec:detection-lit}

The first deep detectors were frame-level convolutional networks. MesoNet \cite{afchar2018mesonet} used a compact network that focuses on mesoscopic image properties, and the FaceForensics++ authors found that an XceptionNet \cite{chollet2017xception} pretrained on ImageNet and fine-tuned on face crops performed best on their benchmark. Such detectors reach very high accuracy on the manipulation methods they are trained on, but they learn method-specific artifacts and lose a large part of their accuracy on other datasets \cite{yan2023deepfakebench}.

A second generation of methods tried to learn artifacts that are common to many manipulations. Face X-ray \cite{li2020facexray} trains a network to locate the blending boundary that most face swaps leave when a generated face is pasted into a frame, and it can be trained on synthetic blends of real faces without any deepfake. Self-blended images \cite{shiohara2022sbi} extend this idea by blending a face with a slightly transformed copy of itself, which produces subtle but general training artifacts. These methods improved cross-dataset results, but they rely on assumptions about how forgeries are made, and fully synthetic faces, which involve no blending at all, violate them.

@section FOUNDATION-MODEL DETECTORS {sec:foundation-lit}

Vision transformers \cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} split an image into patches, embed each patch as a token and process the sequence with self-attention. CLIP \cite{radford2021clip} trains a vision transformer and a text transformer together, so that the embeddings of matching images and captions are close, on hundreds of millions of image and text pairs collected from the internet. The resulting image features describe content at a level that is not specific to any forgery method, which makes them a strong basis for detection. Ojha et al. \cite{ojha2023univfd} showed that even a linear classifier on frozen CLIP features detects images from generators that were not used for training far better than a fully trained convolutional detector.

Several recent detectors adapt such encoders more carefully. Forensics Adapter \cite{cui2025forensicsadapter} adds a lightweight adapter to a frozen CLIP model that learns forgery traces specific to faces. Effort \cite{yan2025effort} decomposes the weights of the encoder with a singular value decomposition and trains only a small residual subspace that is kept orthogonal to the pretrained knowledge. Han et al. \cite{han2025fcg} guide the adaptation of a foundation model with facial components for video-level detection. GenD \cite{yermakov2026gend}, the detector used in this project, shows that a simpler recipe suffices: it trains only the affine parameters of the layer-normalization layers \cite{ba2016layernorm} of a CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder and a linear head, normalizes the features to unit length, and adds the alignment and uniformity objectives of Wang and Isola \cite{wang2020alignuniform}, which pull features of the same class together and spread all features over the hypersphere. GenD also identifies paired genuine and manipulated training videos as essential to avoid shortcut learning. All of these detectors are trained once and evaluated on fixed benchmarks; none of them addresses how the model should be updated afterwards.

@section CONTINUAL LEARNING {sec:cl-lit}

Continual learning studies models that learn from a sequence of tasks without access to the full data of earlier tasks. Its central obstacle is catastrophic forgetting, first described for connectionist networks by McCloskey and Cohen \cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}: gradient updates for a new task overwrite the weights that encoded the old ones. De Lange et al. \cite{delange2022survey} group the methods that counter forgetting into three families.

Regularization-based methods add a penalty that keeps the new model close to the old one. Elastic weight consolidation (EWC) \cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc} estimates the importance of each parameter for earlier tasks with the diagonal of the Fisher information matrix and penalizes changes of important parameters. Learning without forgetting (LwF) \cite{li2018lwf} works in function space instead: during training on new data it applies knowledge distillation \cite{hinton2015distilling}, keeping the softened output distribution of the updated model close to that of the frozen previous model on the same inputs. Feature distillation applies the same idea to intermediate representations, usually on all samples.

Replay-based methods store a small memory of earlier samples, or of their features, and mix them into training. iCaRL \cite{rebuffi2017icarl} keeps exemplars of every class and classifies with the nearest class mean, and semantic drift compensation \cite{yu2020sdc} keeps class prototypes in feature space and estimates how they move as the feature extractor changes, so that no raw samples are needed. Architecture-based methods add parameters or modules for every task. Because this project requires the architecture and the cost of inference to stay unchanged, and allows no stored data, its main candidates are regularization methods; replay and joint training serve only as reference points.

@section CONTINUAL DEEPFAKE DETECTION {sec:cdd-lit}

Continual deepfake detection applies these ideas to a stream of forgery datasets. CoReD \cite{kim2021cored} transfers knowledge between tasks with teacher and student representation distillation. DFIL \cite{pan2023dfil} combines exemplar replay with the learning of domain-invariant forgery clues. HDP \cite{sun2023hdp} preserves the historical feature distribution of earlier tasks, and the aligned feature isolation method of Cheng et al. \cite{cheng2025surlid} isolates the features of successive tasks and aligns them so that new tasks do not overwrite old ones. Prompt2Guard \cite{laiti2024prompt2guard} keeps a CLIP model frozen and learns a set of prompts for each task. Fontana et al. \cite{fontana2025chronological} order datasets chronologically and argue that a detector cannot be expected to generalize to generators that appear far in its future, and Kim et al. \cite{kim2026msfd} preserve spatial and temporal knowledge for continual video detection.

GPL \cite{zhang2025gpl} is the work closest to this project. It explicitly aims to preserve generalization to unseen forgeries during continual updates and stores no images. It keeps the detector frozen and learns a lightweight input watermark, trained with a hyperbolic contrastive loss against cluster centers of the base task, and projects the gradient of each new task so that it does not conflict with the gradient that keeps the model close to its previous outputs. We re-implemented GPL on the same detector and protocol to compare it directly (Section {sec:baselines}).

Most of this work uses ImageNet-pretrained convolutional backbones, and several methods store raw face images of earlier tasks, which raises privacy and licensing concerns. Most of it also measures forgetting on the datasets already learned. The setting of this project starts from a detector that already generalizes, treats the retention of that generalization as the main quantity, and stores no data.

@section CONTINUAL FAKE-AUDIO DETECTION {sec:audio-lit}

Spoofed-speech detection faces the same problem as face forgery detection: new speech synthesis and voice conversion systems keep appearing. Ma et al. \cite{ma2021dfwf} proposed Detecting Fake Without Forgetting (DFWF), which adds two terms to the classification loss: an LwF term and a positive sample alignment that keeps the embeddings of genuine utterances under the current model close, by cosine similarity, to those under the previous model. The motivation was that genuine speech is consistent across recording conditions, while spoofing attacks differ from one another. DFWF stores no old data and was evaluated on attacks of the ASVspoof 2019 challenge that the model had already learned. Later audio methods modify the direction of the weight updates instead, and treat the compact genuine class and the diverse fake classes differently \cite{zhang2023rawm,zhang2024rwm}.

The update objective studied in this project is DFWF's positive sample alignment, transferred to a visual foundation-model detector. Its contribution is therefore not the objective itself but a controlled answer, for visual face forgery detection, to whether and why the objective preserves detection of forgeries that are never trained, and how it compares with specialized visual methods.

Three gaps emerge from this survey. First, continual deepfake detection has mostly been evaluated on the datasets that the detector has already learned, so the effect of updates on the detection of forgeries outside the stream, which is the main asset of a generalizable detector, is largely unmeasured. Second, almost all of this work uses convolutional backbones trained on ImageNet, and it is not known how parameter-efficient foundation-model detectors such as GenD behave when they are updated. Third, genuine-sample alignment has been proposed only for audio and evaluated only on previously learned attacks; whether it protects generalization in a visual detector, whether the genuine class is really the reason, and how it compares with GPL under the same protocol have not been tested. This project addresses these three gaps.
